# Practice Room improvement plan

## Purpose and entry

Give a child who chooses to practice a short musical idea they can hear, copy, and eventually play with the farm band. A successful first visit can be as small as hearing one sound and making it back. A child who already knows the room can go straight to a phrase, a rhythm exercise, or the band.

Keep double-tap on the avatar or its equipped instrument as the landscape entrance at every age. Keep single-tap playback, keyboard and assistive activation, and the existing caregiver-help shortcut. Preserve the current opt-in discovery rules, including the age and equipping conditions for practice hints and the caregiver's ability to offer one explicitly. The room should remain a quiet discovery in the landscape. Adapt the experience once the child opens it.

The audit found three issues that should drive the first implementation. The first Simple piano exercise was a two-note group, and Simple guitar opened with a three-note group plus fingering charts. On a 780 × 284 CSS px phone, stage tabs were 22 px high, transport controls 27 px, and guitar string lanes 21 px. In Rhythm, the phrase track could mark a note missed while the keys stayed lit and the message still said “Your turn.” Keep the live instrument highlights and self-paced copying that already work well.

## One musical action at a time

| Activity | What the child does | What the room does |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Hear it** | Presses one large play button and hears a sound or short phrase. | Plays one pass with synchronized highlights on the real instrument and a clear ending. Offer **Hear it again** and **My turn** as soon as that pass ends. Save the count-in for activities that require entering in time. |
| **Copy it** | Plays one highlighted action at a time, at their own pace. | Starts with one sound, then offers a short sequence. Mark the sound that worked and point to what remains. Offer a replay of the current action and let the child return to the full example at any time. |
| **Tap the rhythm** | Listens to an example, then copies its rhythm on one large pad. A caregiver can also clap or pat along. | Starts with a steady pulse and then uses the rhythm of the same phrase. Give a visible and audible lead-in to the child's turn. End after one response and offer another try or an easier repeat. The pad uses a consistent sound through the existing audio engine. |
| **Play with the band** | Joins the accompaniment freely or chooses to try the phrase in time. | Starts with unscored playing over quieter backing. Stop is always visible. Offer an optional **Phrase challenge** with a lead-in, the real instrument, and a clear end to each attempt. Keep the existing phrase library and repeat tools available through the activity chooser or practice tools. |

The default path follows that order, with **Hear it again**, **Choose an activity**, and the exit available when relevant. One activity's score never controls access to another. Finishing a short phrase offers a small confirmation and a choice to repeat, try the next activity, or leave. Offer an **I play, you answer** prompt in caregiver help so an adult can model a sound, clap the beat, or swap turns at the same device.

## Age, settings, and difficulty

| Profile age | First view in the default room | What is available when they want more |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Under 3** | Shared-play view with one sound to hear and one large target to try. Use the child's equipped instrument when it has a straightforward action and an explicitly labeled practice pad when it needs a compound gesture. Give the caregiver a short turn-taking prompt on request. | The activity chooser gives a caregiver access to guided and full practice. |
| **3–4** | One audible example followed by untimed copying. Begin with a single tap or pluck and offer a second distinct sound after the first round. Keep the demonstration replayable and use a hand cue where the gesture needs explanation. | A short rhythm echo, unscored band play, and the full room through the chooser. |
| **5–7** | A short **Hear it → Copy it** activity with two or three actions once a single action is familiar. Offer **Tap the rhythm** after copying. | Direct access to rhythm, band play, phrase choice, and the timed challenge. |
| **8+** | One recommended exercise with immediate access to the activity and phrase selectors. The equipped instrument remains the starting instrument. | Quick access to tempo, repeats, notation or charts according to saved settings, and timed feedback. |

- **Age sets the opening view.** Any age can choose guided or full practice inside the room. Use session-only choices initially. Consider a saved per-child room preference only if observed use shows that families repeatedly switch on entry.
- **Saved instrument complexity sets the phrase library and the normal instrument surface.** Guided activities can present a smaller authored exercise and a focused set of real controls within that tier. Saved Advanced settings still allow a one-action start, and an older beginner on Simple still gets the easier repertoire. Any focused view uses the same pitches, fingerings, and sounding controls as the full instrument.
- **Use the saved display preferences.** Respect note-label, playback-label, fingering-chart, and fret-dot choices. In guided views, put enabled technical details behind **Show details** where they would crowd the primary action. Keep spoken or accessible control names and visual action cues available independent of the displayed notation. Keep caregiver familiarity for help language.
- **Increase one demand at a time.** Extend the phrase, vary its rhythm, or introduce a new gesture separately. For keyboards and direct-touch instruments, start with one target. For strings, guide a single pluck with the exercise shape already selected, then teach choosing or holding a chord and finally sweeping. For trumpet, model one embouchure position before adding valve coordination. Where a dedicated beginner exercise is needed, author it against the canonical note and fingering data for the enabled instruments and scene keys. Keep selections deterministic and allow immediate returns to an easier exercise or a replay.

## Visual design and feedback

- **Give the instrument most of the room.** Build the guided view around the illustrated instrument, one short prompt, one large primary action, and the always-available door. Reuse the farm palette and artwork. Move duplicate instrument shortcuts, phrase details, metronome, and technical readouts into the activity chooser or practice tools. Keep necessary control names. Use the extra width on large layouts to place guidance next to the instrument.
- **Show the action at the target.** Use a high-contrast outline or shape marker plus a brief hand demonstration when helpful. Give “listen,” “get ready,” “play,” and “held” distinct visual states. A short sequence can use one recognizable marker per action, with a clear current marker and checkmarks for completed actions. Pair symbols with short labels and, where useful, an optional brief spoken cue that leaves room to hear the model. Provide a static cue for reduced motion and equivalents for keyboard and assistive technology. Expose the current instruction separately from the actual pressed state of a control.
- **Design compact landscape before adding detail.** Aim for at least 44 × 44 CSS px on primary controls and guided targets. Keep the door, primary action, and playable surface clear of safe-area insets. Fix the edge sharp and low kick drum. For dense full instrument surfaces, use more available height, fewer guided targets, or a deliberate view change before shrinking the active targets. Inspect visible hit regions and spacing with native touch, especially when decorative fretwork or overlapping keys make bounding boxes misleading. [FarmJam visual standards](visual-standards.md), [W3C enhanced target size](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/target-size-enhanced.html)
- **Make timing cues and feedback agree.** Preview the next action before the beat, mark the onset clearly, and end the invitation to play when its accepted window ends. If useful, show a separate hold cue for sustained sounds. After a miss, keep the next action obvious with **Hear it again** or **Try this beat**. Give timing-specific feedback only when the app can distinguish timing from pitch or a partial chord. Keep numeric scores in the optional challenge details and use the same scoring unit for progress, summaries, and grouped actions. Judge timing from the shared audio clock. Treat tempo, tolerance, and count-in length as defaults to test on real devices.

## Implementation order

1. **Guided entry and layout.** Derive the opening activity and visible details from the existing age and instrument settings. Build the shared guided layout, one-pass Hear it, and the first untimed Copy it exercises. Keep the existing full tools reachable inside the room throughout development. Fix the compact hit regions in this milestone. Reuse the existing `PracticeRoomLayout`, `PracticeRoomHeader`, `InstrumentSurface`, and passive gesture components. Keep responsive behavior in `practice.css` and make the guide use the same layout and controls. [Architecture](architecture.md)
2. **Rhythm and recovery.** Add the one-pad echo and correct the timed cue lifecycle. In the practice logic, distinguish preview, count-in, example, response, and completed or stopped states. Have the audio engine schedule each phase and report authoritative beat and onset events. A repeat, instrument switch, help interruption, blur, or exit must cancel the previous run and release its inputs. Require an explicit resume after interruption. Keep the current full phrase exercises and their authored voicings available. [Musical reference](musical-reference.md)
3. **Band play and caregiver guidance.** Add unscored accompaniment and keep the timed phrase challenge selectable. Trim live feedback to the current action so it leaves the instrument visible. Verify that Stop and the door release backing and manual notes, and exiting returns the original farm playback, equipment, tempo, sound settings, and focus. Update contextual help, the practice hint's in-room wording, and the versioned caregiver guide with silent previews of the live room controls. Keep any adaptive state inside the room session for this implementation. [Caregiver guide](caregiver-guide.md), [Storage and updates](storage-and-updates.md)

## Acceptance and evaluation

- **Entry and profile behavior.** Test a single landscape tap, double-tap on avatar and equipped instrument, keyboard entry, the caregiver shortcut, and the existing age-aware hint rules. Exercise each opening view, the guided/full override, and an older saved profile with labels and charts off. Verify profile switches, retries, and exits preserve each child's original settings.
- **Musical progression and recovery.** Verify a single Listen pass, repeat, untimed copying, rhythm without pitch choice, and an unscored band session. In the challenge, exercise an early tap, a missed tap while a hold remains visible, a wrong pitch, a partial chord, and replay. Test interruption during count-in, active playback, and a held input. Check that the live room and the silent guide remain visually consistent.
- **Layout and access.** Check desktop, tablet, and 780 × 284 compact landscape, plus the minimum supported layout and safe areas. Exercise representative keys, pads, strings, and winds, and check reachability across the enabled catalog. Verify focus order and restoration, readable cue contrast, reduced motion, and screen-reader instructions. Run the smallest relevant practice, timing, ownership, learning, and guide-demonstration checks for each milestone. [Contributing checks](contributing.md)
- **Observed use.** Compare the current and guided experiences with caregiver consent on a phone and tablet. Start a baseline comparison inside the room so the intentionally hidden landscape entrance stays outside the usability target. Begin with caregiver-child pairs under 3 and children ages 3–4 and 5–7, and include older beginners to check access to full tools. Observe time and adult prompts needed to make the first intended sound, whether children understand whose turn it is, accidental touches, and what they do after a miss. Try a short echo by ear after a guided round, and listen for cue clarity and latency on real hardware. Use the observed behavior to tune defaults. Treat any claim about lasting learning as a separate evaluation question.

## Research behind the design

- Playful training with singing, movement, percussion, and listening improved rhythmic imitation in a 2026 study of two- and four-year-olds. Synchronization continues developing through primary school. These findings support starting with shared listening and imitation and offering timed coordination progressively. [Early-childhood training study](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10643-026-02247-z), [Middle-childhood synchronization study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38977822/)
- Preschool touchscreen experiments support adult modeling for the youngest children and concrete on-screen demonstrations and audio prompts as children become ready. Research on a preschool vocabulary app supports testing gradual challenges and actionable early hints in FarmJam. [Touchscreen prompts study](https://faculty.washington.edu/alexisr/TouchscreenPrompts.pdf), [App feedback and difficulty experiment](https://doi.org/10.1111/bjet.13055)
- Preschool drumming with a human partner supported synchronization in an experimental task. Music-education guidance also emphasizes listening, improvising, movement, and social music-making. This supports giving caregiver turn-taking and free band play a place alongside structured imitation. [Joint drumming experiment](https://scholars.duke.edu/publication/1458233), [NAfME early childhood music guidance](https://nafme.org/resource/early-childhood-music-education/)
